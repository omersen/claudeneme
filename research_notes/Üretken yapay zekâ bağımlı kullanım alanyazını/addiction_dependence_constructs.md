## Part A. Verification of references already cited in the drafts, and of the drafts' claims about them

### Takeaway
All seven references exist, and the bibliographic data in the drafts matches what was found. Most of the drafts' claims are accurate. Five sentences need correcting or softening:
- (1) The statement that AI-addiction scales are "largely based on Griffiths (2005)" is overstated. Ciudad-Fernández et al. say the scales are framed after substance-use-disorder criteria, and only some of them use the components model.
- (2) The gloss on Ciudad-Fernández's "circular confirmatory approach" is looser than the authors' own definition.
- (3) Caplan's (2010) deficient self-regulation is cognitive preoccupation plus compulsive use. It is not "kullanım sıklığı" (frequency of use).
- (4) Zhang et al. (2024) did not study "değerlendirme çabası" (evaluation effort).
- (5) The "AICD (Zhang et al., 2024)" that Ciudad-Fernández criticise is a different paper from Zhang, Zhao, Zhou and Kim.

### Cited Findings

**A1. Griffiths (2005). Metadata VERIFIED.**
- Summary: The paper proposes that all addictions, whether to substances or behaviours, share six components: salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict and relapse. It places addiction in a biopsychosocial framework. A widely quoted line holds that healthy enthusiasms add to life whereas addictions take away from it. — [T&F landing page, J Subst Use 10(4)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14659890500114359); [NTU IRep](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/7171/). The six components are reproduced in secondary full text: [Vadlin et al., 2015, Scand J Psychol](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/sjop.12196) and [Kim et al., 2025, Consumer Psychology Review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1002/arcp.70008).
- Kim et al. (2025) explicitly use Griffiths (2005) to frame "AI addiction". This shows that transferring the model to GenAI is actually happening in the literature. — [Kim et al., 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1002/arcp.70008)
- Draft v39 (line 10) lists the six components in Turkish: "dikkat çekicilik, duygu durum değişimi, tolerans, yoksunluk, çatışma ve nüksetme". **Correct.**
- **Flag.** Draft v39 (line 10) says "Klinik anlamdaki yapay zekâ bağımlılığı, büyük ölçüde Griffiths'in (2005) … modeline dayanır." This is overstated. Ciudad-Fernández et al. (2025) describe the four existing ChatGPT-addiction scales as "all framed after substance use disorder criteria". The scales also have different origins:
  - PUCAI-6 was adapted from the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, which is based on the components model.
  - PCUS-11 was based on the DSM-5 criteria for Internet gaming disorder.
  - GAA-3 took items from a WeChat-addiction measure.
  - AICD was built from earlier technology and AI dependence questionnaires.
  - Sources: [Ciudad-Fernández et al., 2025, via UNIL/ResearchGate/ScienceDirect search result](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389698665_People_are_not_becoming_AIholic_Questioning_the_ChatGPT_addiction_construct); [Consensus abstract](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0235547a1fa95867a14103ed8aac184c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
  - Suggested wording: "madde kullanım bozukluğu ölçütlerine ve bu ölçütlerden türetilen Griffiths'in (2005) bileşenler modeline dayanır."
  - "Klinik anlamdaki" also suggests a recognised diagnosis exists, and none does. Vasiliu (2026) explicitly does not treat problematic GenAI-chatbot use as "a formally established diagnostic entity". — [Vasiliu, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1816917/full)

**A2. Billieux, Schimmenti, Khazaal, Maurage and Heeren (2015). Metadata VERIFIED (JBA 4(3), 119–123).**
- Summary: Recent publications suggest that almost any daily activity can become a genuine addiction. The authors argue that atheoretical, confirmatory research approaches produce "an unlimited list of 'new' behavioral addictions". They warn that overpathologizing everyday activities will lead to a dismissive appraisal of behavioural-addiction research. Their roadmap moves the field from a criteria-based approach towards one that studies the psychological processes involved. The paper drew a set of JBA commentaries. — [UCLouvain record](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/entities/publication/8485f90a-056a-4da8-8ef6-f3558a076223); [PubMed 26014667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26014667/); [one of the commentaries, JBA 4(3)](https://www.akjournals.com/view/journals/2006/4/3/article-p139.xml)
- Draft v39 (line 10) says "gündelik davranışların bu yolla gereğinden fazla patolojik sayılmasını eleştirdi". Line 185 and the ITALL sentence also match. **Accurate.**

**A3. Kardefelt-Winther et al. (2017). Metadata VERIFIED (Addiction 112(10), 1709–1715; online 15 Feb 2017). Claim VERIFIED from full text.**
- The proposed operational definition, quoted verbatim: "A repeated behaviour leading to significant harm or distress. The behaviour is not reduced by the person and persists over a significant period of time. The harm or distress is of a functionally impairing nature." — [Scholar Gateway full text, Addiction 112(10)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/add.13763)
- The authors want the definition to rest on two components only: "(a) significant functional impairment or distress as a direct consequence of the behaviour and (b) persistence over time." For withdrawal, tolerance and conflict, they suggest "not using these symptoms when operationalizing the definition unless more evidence is provided". — [Kardefelt-Winther et al., 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/add.13763)
- They give four exclusion criteria. **Exclusion criterion 3** is directly useful for the "yoğun fakat işlevsel kullanım" argument: a behaviour should not be conceptualised as addiction if it "can be characterized as a period of prolonged intensive involvement that detracts time and focus from other aspects of life, but does not lead to significant functional impairment or distress". — [Kardefelt-Winther et al., 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/add.13763)
- They recommend a person-centred approach built on qualitative studies. In their words: "Only in a second step should items be developed and assessment instruments evaluated in terms of psychometric properties." They also quote Clark and Watson: a good theory articulates "not only what a construct is, but also what it is not". — [Kardefelt-Winther et al., 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/add.13763)
- Draft v39 (line 10) says "davranışsal bağımlılığın işlevsel bozulma ölçütü olmadan tanımlanmamasını önerdi". **Accurate.** It could be made more precise by adding "ve zaman içinde süreklilik". The qualitative-first recommendation also supports the method of the present article directly.

**A4. Caplan (2010). Metadata consistent across secondary sources (CHB 26(5), 1089–1097). DOI not resolved directly.**
- Summary: The GPIUS2 has 15 items. It covers preference for online social interaction (POSI), mood regulation, **deficient self-regulation** and negative outcomes. Deficient self-regulation is a second-order construct made up of cognitive preoccupation and compulsive Internet use. In the model, POSI and mood regulation predict deficient self-regulation, which in turn predicts negative outcomes. — [search summary of GPIUS2 validation literature; Polish validation in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10654343/); [Japanese validation, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0273895)
- Draft v39 (line 11) lists five components ("tercihli çevrimiçi sosyal etkileşim, duygu durum düzenleme, bilişsel meşguliyet, kompulsif kullanım ve olumsuz sonuçlar") and says regulation difficulty and negative outcomes are "ayrı fakat ilişkili". **Accurate.** It would be more precise to say that deficient self-regulation *predicts* negative outcomes.
- **Flag, v39 line 11.** "Problemli kullanım kavramı, işlevsel bozulmayı merkeze alan daha yumuşak bir çerçeve sunar" does not describe Caplan's model precisely. The model is cognitive-behavioural and centres on POSI and deficient self-regulation. Negative outcomes are its outcome variable, not its centre. Suggested: "problemli kullanımı düzenleme güçlüğü ve bunun yol açtığı olumsuz sonuçlar üzerinden tanımlayan bilişsel-davranışçı bir çerçeve".
- **Flag, v39 line 18.** "Kullanım sıklığı ve kullanım süresinin uzaması … Caplan'ın (2010) modelinde düzenleme güçlüğü bileşenine karşılık gelir" is inaccurate as written. Caplan's deficient self-regulation means cognitive preoccupation plus compulsive use, that is, difficulty controlling one's use. Frequency or amount of use is not a GPIUS2 component. Suggested: "planlanandan uzun sürme ve kullanımı kontrol etmede güçlük … Caplan'ın (2010) modelinde kompulsif kullanıma, dolayısıyla düzenleme güçlüğüne karşılık gelir; kullanım sıklığı ise modelde ayrı bir bileşen değildir."

**A5. Ciudad-Fernández, von Hammerstein and Billieux (2025). Metadata VERIFIED (Addictive Behaviors 166, Article 108325; available online 9 March 2025; PubMed 40073725). Both claims VERIFIED.**
- Summary: At least four ChatGPT-addiction scales exist: PUCAI-6 (Hu et al., 2023), PCUS-11 (Yu et al., 2024), GAA-3 (Zhou and Zhang, 2024) and AICD (Zhang et al., 2024). All are "framed after substance use disorder criteria". The authors say that labelling a behaviour as addictive requires "convincing evidence of negative consequences, impaired control, psychological distress, and functional impairment", and that the existing research on problematic ChatGPT use does not provide it. Students' reported complaints, such as laziness, lower creativity and less critical thinking, "hardly fit" clinically significant harm. They warn against (over)pathologization, unnecessary treatment and excessive regulation. — [Consensus abstract](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0235547a1fa95867a14103ed8aac184c/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [search result summarising UNIL/ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S030646032500084X)
- **"Circular confirmatory approach" (VERIFIED).** The authors define it as "reusing items from already existing scales that assess addictive or compulsive online activities, which have themselves been developed by recycling items originally intended to assess substance use disorders." — [UNIL open-access copy, per search result](https://api.unil.ch/iris/server/api/core/bitstreams/48569b25-9ee4-4284-ab38-f969e69b2f09/content)
- **Tolerance and withdrawal (VERIFIED).** The scales assess symptoms such as "tolerance" or "withdrawal" that "have, to date, never been empirically studied in the context of problematic or 'addictive' use of generative AI chatbots". — [UNIL copy, per search result](https://iris.unil.ch/bitstreams/48569b25-9ee4-4284-ab38-f969e69b2f09/download)

### Inferences
- Taken together, Kardefelt-Winther et al. (2017), Ciudad-Fernández et al. (2025) and Oleas et al. (2026) make one argument across three levels. The first sets the general rule: functional impairment is required, and tolerance and withdrawal should not be used without evidence. The second applies that rule to GenAI measurement. The third offers an empirical alternative, "externalization rather than addiction". Citing them together gives the article's "bağımlı kullanım ≠ klinik bağımlılık" position a strong basis.
- Kardefelt-Winther's exclusion criterion 3 is the most direct published support for the drafts' rule that "yoğun fakat işlevsel kullanım" falls outside the construct. It should be cited at v39 lines 12, 185 and 191, alongside Billieux (2015) and Ciudad-Fernández (2025).

## Part B1. Scales and constructs of AI/ChatGPT/GenAI addiction, dependence and problematic use (2023–2026), and critiques

### Takeaway
Since 2023 a fast-growing set of instruments has appeared: PUCAI, PCUS, GAA, AICD, DIA, GAID, PCGUS, RAIAS and others. Most are unidimensional or borrow addiction symptoms, and several explicitly include withdrawal (for example, GAID's "withdrawal" factor). Critiques from the addiction field (Ciudad-Fernández et al., 2025; Montag et al., 2025; Vasiliu, 2026) and from psychometrics (Oleas et al., 2026) argue that the construct lacks evidence of clinically significant harm. They propose seeing it as a problem of cognitive and self-regulatory externalization rather than addiction.

### Cited Findings
- **Hu, Mao and Kim (2023)** introduced "problematic use of conversational AI" (PUCAI). Social anxiety predicted PUCAI through loneliness and rumination, and mind perception intensified the link. According to Ciudad-Fernández et al., the PUCAI-6 items were adapted from the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale. — [ACM DL record, CHB 145](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1016/j.chb.2023.107760); [CityUHK Scholars](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/how-social-anxiety-leads-to-problematic-use-of-conversational-ai-)
- **Yu, Chen and Yang (2024)** developed the Problematic ChatGPT Use Scale (PCUS) with Taiwanese adults. EFA and CFA supported its structure, and usage time and depression correlated positively with scores. Per Ciudad-Fernández et al., its 11 items were based on DSM-5 Internet gaming disorder criteria. — [Springer, Current Psychology 43](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-024-06259-z)
- **Zhang, Yin, Zhang, Li and Li (2025)** built the AI Chatbot Dependence scale from literature and in-depth interviews. From 17 initial items, a single factor with eight items remained. — [SAGE, CBSN 28(2)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1089/cyber.2024.0240)
- **Morales-García, Sairitupa-Sanchez, Morales-García and Morales-García (2024)** developed the Scale for Dependence on AI (DIA) with 528 university students. EFA gave a unifactorial structure, CFA confirmed it, and the scale worked consistently for men and women. The framing concern was that excessive reliance on AI may reduce cognitive skills and motivation. This is the scale Savaş (2024) adapted into Turkish (see B4). — [Frontiers in Education record](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1323898/text); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/ae5671cd680b4332a21a79447e550198)
- **Goh, Hartanto and Majeed (2025)** developed the Generative AI Dependency Scale across six studies in the United States and Singapore. It has three factors: *cognitive preoccupation, negative consequences, withdrawal*. Scores were associated with procrastination, cognitive failures, lower critical thinking, and lower need satisfaction. Note that it still contains a withdrawal factor, the very symptom Ciudad-Fernández et al. say has not been studied empirically in the GenAI context. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S245195882500260X); [PsyArXiv/OSF preprint](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/aphtb); [Sciety DOI record](https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.1016/j.chbr.2025.100845)
- **Maral, Naycı, Bilmez, Erdemir and Satıcı** tested the Problematic ChatGPT Use Scale (PCGUS) in Turkish samples. It has 9 items and one dimension; CFA and IRT supported it, and measurement invariance held at the configural, metric and scalar levels. Scores correlated positively with AI addiction, Internet gaming disorder and Internet addiction, and negatively with conscientiousness. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c82496ab9cf25a05839ef823dbd5afc2/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-025-01509-y); [YTU AVESİS](https://avesis.yildiz.edu.tr/yayin/4d633433-efeb-4a0e-b586-51b387b78ed8/problematic-chatgpt-use-scale-ai-human-collaboration-or-unraveling-the-dark-side-of-chatgpt)
- **El-Sayed, Alsenany, Asal, Alasqah and Gutiérrez-Puertas (2025)** developed a 22-item "AI Addiction Scale for Researchers" (RAIAS) with nursing researchers, using a literature review, interviews, EFA and CFA. The framing blends "overreliance" and "addictive behaviors", which illustrates how loosely the terms are used. — [Wiley full text via Scholar Gateway](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1155/jonm/8458533)
- **Ye et al. (2024)** studied ChatGPT dependence among Taiwanese university students using the I-PACE model. Inert thinking and positive ChatGPT experiences were associated with dependence, and so was avoidance learning motivation. — [Springer, Education and Information Technologies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-024-12966-8)
- **Critique: Montag, Yang, Wu, Ali and Elhai (2025).** This short communication in *Annals of the NYAS* examines "terms such as ChatGPT addiction" critically, from the standpoint of the Internet-use-disorder field. — [Wiley](https://nyaspubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/nyas.15337); [Consensus abstract](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8c5c95462551561bb4d360cd26f1a847/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- **Critique/review: Vasiliu (2026).** This narrative review covers the concepts, epidemiology, risk factors and assessment instruments for problematic use of GenAI chatbots. It deliberately does not advance the condition "as a formally established diagnostic entity". — [Frontiers in Public Health](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1816917/full)
- **Counterpoint (pro-addiction framing): Yankouskaya et al. (2025).** This viewpoint argues that personalised responses, emotional validation and continuous engagement can foster dependency. It says these tendencies "align with known features of addiction, such as increased tolerance and conflict". — [Springer, Human-Centric Intelligent Systems](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44230-025-00090-w)
- **Conceptual bridge: Kim, Usman, Garvey and Duhachek (2025).** They cross two dimensions, *Division of Cognitive Labor* and *Metacognitive Oversight*, to get four patterns: Skilled Augmentation, Managed Automation, Unguided Effort and **Cognitive Surrender**. Cognitive Surrender is where users hand over both cognitive execution and metacognitive control to AI. They *hypothesise* a drift "from rational efficiency-seeking through dependency to behavioral addiction". — [Scholar Gateway, Consumer Psychology Review 9(1)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1002/arcp.70008)

## Part B2. Overreliance on AI versus appropriate reliance

### Takeaway
Human–AI interaction research defines overreliance by outcome: the user accepts *incorrect* AI output. Appropriate reliance means accepting correct outputs and rejecting incorrect ones. Newer education research adds process ("strategy-graded") reliance. It shows that reliance is not inherently problematic; what matters is whether judgment and control stay with the learner. This supports defining "bağımlı kullanım" by what is delegated and how it is evaluated, not by how much the tool is used.

### Cited Findings
- **Passi and Vorvoreanu (2022)** reviewed about 60 papers from HCI, Human Factors, IUI, CSCW, organisational science and FAccT. Overreliance on AI occurs "when users start accepting incorrect AI outputs". The report covers how overreliance arises, how it is measured, what it leads to, and how to mitigate it. — [Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/overreliance-on-ai-literature-review/); [PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Aether-Overreliance-on-AI-Review-Final-6.21.22.pdf)
- **Passi, Dhanorkar and Vorvoreanu (2024)** synthesised about 50 papers for GenAI. The goal they describe is fostering appropriate reliance, meaning users "accept correct AI outputs and reject incorrect ones", and avoiding both overreliance and underreliance. — [Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/appropriate-reliance-on-generative-ai-research-synthesis/); [executive summary PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/GenAI_AppropriateReliance_Published2024-3-21.pdf)
- **Parasuraman and Riley (1997)** separated use, misuse, disuse and abuse of automation. **"Misuse" = overreliance on automation**, which leads to monitoring failures and decision biases; disuse is underreliance. — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/001872097778543886); [PubMed review of the article](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18689046/)
- **Zhai, Wibowo and Li (2024)** is a systematic review of how students' over-reliance on AI dialogue systems affects decision-making, critical thinking and analytical reasoning. It describes overreliance as accepting AI recommendations without question when users cannot judge the AI's reliability. — [Springer PDF per search](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40561-024-00316-7.pdf); [ERIC EJ1428486](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=ieee&ff1=subTechnology+Uses+in+Education&id=EJ1428486)
- **Hou, Zhu and Sudarshan (2025)** criticise *outcome-graded* reliance measures, such as switch fraction and weight of advice, as too narrow for problem-solving. They propose *strategy-graded*, process-oriented reliance, and distinguish reflective, cautious, collaborative and thoughtless use. Critical-thinking skills and dispositions were linked with reflective and cautious use. "High trust in AI does not necessarily lead to overreliance." — [Scholar Gateway full text, BJET 56(5)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/bjet.13613)
- **Wu and Lu (2026)** start from Bandura's proxy agency: "such reliance is not inherently problematic … The central question is where supportive delegation ends and displacement of learner control begins." They followed 342 undergraduates in a collaborative writing course over three waves. In an RI-CLPM, higher-than-usual reliance predicted lower subsequent perceived agency, and the reverse path was not supported. — [Scholar Gateway, BJET (early view)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/bjet.70090)
- **Oleas et al. (2026)** say overreliance, as opposed to adaptive AI-assisted learning, "would require evidence of functional interference, reduced self-regulation, or poorer academic outcomes". — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13433740/)
- **Elzeky, Alanazi, Shahine and Atashzadeh-Shoorideh (2026)** studied nursing students cross-sectionally. Reliance on ChatGPT was associated with lower academic self-efficacy, and through it with lower self-directed learning and more problem-solving difficulties. — [Scholar Gateway, Nursing Forum](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1155/nuf/3301894)

## Part B3. Academic misconduct with GenAI as a separate construct (supports excluding "misuse")

### Takeaway
Academic-integrity research treats unauthorised GenAI use as a form of cheating or plagiarism: a rule violation handled through policy, assessment design and detection. Its explanatory frameworks (for example the fraud diamond of pressure, opportunity, rationalization and capability) differ from those of dependence or self-regulation. This supports keeping "yanlış kullanım" outside the "bağımlı kullanım" construct.

### Cited Findings
- **Cotton, Cotton and Shipway (2024)** discuss the chances and risks of ChatGPT in higher education, how hard it is to detect and prevent academic dishonesty, and institutional strategies: policies and procedures, training and support, and detection methods. — [ERIC EJ1415334](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1415334); [Plymouth research portal](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/chatting-and-cheating-ensuring-academic-integrity-in-the-era-of-c-3/)
- **Perkins (2023)** examines the academic-integrity implications of students using LLMs in formal assessments. Such tools can produce "original, coherent text" that escapes detection by software and by trained staff. — [ERIC EJ1382355](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1382355)
- **Kofinas, Tsay and Pike (2025)** ran experiments at two UK universities. Markers generally could not tell apart assessments with GenAI input. The study frames GenAI as a way to "circumvent assessment approaches", which is an integrity problem. — [Scholar Gateway, BJET 56(6)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1111/bjet.13585)
- **Campbell, Frawley, Lambie, Cabrera and Vizcarra (2025)** analysed program and university policies in counsellor education. They report that "references to unauthorised use of generative AI were most often referred to as academic dishonesty or plagiarism". — [Scholar Gateway, Counselling and Psychotherapy Research 25(1)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1002/capr.12880)
- **Sangka, Pratama, Hidayat and Fashoto (2025)** model GPT-related academic fraud (plagiarism, exam cheating, unethical collaboration) with the fraud diamond, with AI as a moderator. They surveyed Indonesian students. The construct is theorised as fraud, not dependence. — [Scholar Gateway, Human Behavior and Emerging Technologies](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/ai/10.1155/hbe2/7118205)

## Part B4. Turkish studies: AI dependence/addiction scales and studies with university students and pre-service teachers

### Takeaway
Turkish research uses mostly unidimensional "yapay zekâ bağımlılığı" instruments: the DIA adapted by Savaş (2024), and the PCGUS by Maral et al. It mainly reports levels and correlates with university students. One new Turkish-developed three-factor scale includes a "Bilişsel Zayıflama" (cognitive weakening) dimension that partly overlaps the article's cognitive and competence dimensions. None of these studies separates dependent use from intensive functional use, which is the gap the article fills.

### Cited Findings
- **Savaş (2024)** adapted the Morales-García et al. (2024) DIA into Turkish with 584 university students, in stages of translation, EFA/CFA, and item validity and reliability. EFA gave a **unidimensional** structure explaining 58.955% of the variance, with Cronbach's α = .82 and test–retest = .79. — [DergiPark JSAR](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/jsar/article/1509301); [PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/4040148)

Distress and self-control mediated the link between problematic ChatGPT use and well-being. The TOAD registry lists a "Problemli ChatGPT Kullanımı Ölçeği" and reports r = .517 with a Yapay Zekâ Bağımlılığı Ölçeği. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-025-01509-y); [TOAD registry](https://toad.halileksi.net/olcek/problemli-chatgpt-kullanimi-olcegi-2/)
